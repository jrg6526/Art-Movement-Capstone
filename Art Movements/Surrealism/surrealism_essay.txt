In 1917, Guillaume Apollinaire – a poet from France – wrote an introduction to a play, coining a word to describe something “beyond reality”: surrealism. Years went by, and in 1924, writer Andre Breton published “The Surrealist Manifesto” - a work in favor of expression through automatism [1][2]. Breton wrote about the possibility of reaching the unconscious mind’s outer limits to conceive irrationality in artworks [2], such as strange juxtapositions of landscapes and objects or completely made-up shapes. 

There are different techniques for creating surrealist artworks. One technique is automatic drawing, which lets the artist draw freely without destruction. Rubbing or scraping the canvas are other practices, both using canvas marks as portions of the artwork. An exquisite corpse involves folding paper, drawing one portion of a figure's body, and passing it to the next artist to connect the hints of the previous drawing [3]. 

The forms of surrealism are diverse; some works are more abstract, and some are more grounded. Many artworks by Jean Arp are made up of vague, uncommon shapes; even Andre Masson and Max Ernst practiced this procedure in some of their paintings. The paintings of Rene Magritte and Salvador Dali are more defined in juxtapositions [3], such as a bird cage in place of a man's upper body and melting clocks in a natural landscape, respectively. 

The Surrealist movement also had its share of female artists. Leonora Carrington painted dream-like voids filled with occult imagery [2][3], such as crystal balls, ghouls, and goats. Meret Oppenheim made physical Surrealist art pieces like “Object”, depicting a teacup, a plate, and a spoon covered with fur. The paintings of Dorothea Tanning typically contain surreal landscapes with eldritch creatures [2][3], such as an unidentified hairy creature with wings. 

All in all, surrealism allows artists to let their minds roam in the vast hills and valleys of imagination that correspond to familiarity in the real world. Ranging from elephants with unusually long and bony legs, to giant sunflower heads in a hallway, to disjointed and indefinite shapes, surrealist artworks tend to follow the recognizable flow of the subconscious and unconscious. The exploration of non-conventional worlds in art still holds up today.
