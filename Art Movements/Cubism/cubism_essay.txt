The year was 1907. An artist by the name of Pablo Picasso showed one of his paintings (<i>Les Demoiselles d’Avignon</i>) to his fellow artist, Georges Braque, displaying five women in an unconventional art style. The painting’s erratic body shapes and faces resembling masks took inspiration from African tribal art, which Picasso viewed at the Paris Trocadero Museum [1][2]. 

Braque – who was interested in <i>Les Demoiselles</i> – started working closely with Picasso to produce more artworks in that style, and in 1908, Braque painted <i>Large Nude</i>, a Cubist piece that included art techniques inspired by Post-Impressionist artist Paul Cezanne. Critic Louis Vauxcelles coined the term “Cubism” as a descriptor for George Braque’s artwork [1]. 

Cubism is defined by a bunch of colorful geometric shapes – in the form of figures – typically arranged in such a way that disjointed perspectives take form [1][2][3]. Take Roger de La Fresnaye’s <i>The Conquest of the Air</i> for example. It depicts two men sitting at a table with the French flag behind them and the sky as the background. (The artwork represents Roger and his brother in a hot air balloon [1].) The painting is made up mostly of simple geometric shapes like triangles, circles, and quadrilaterals. Certain portions give off different viewpoints, like one of the man’s legs facing left while his torso faces forward, and the flag waving on the same level as one of the clouds [3]. 

Besides Pablo and Braque, there are multiple notable Cubists throughout the 20th century. Roger de la Fresnaye's paintings combined Cubism and Post-Impressionism, showing human figures in colorful landscapes. Robert Delaunay created vibrant artworks influenced by technological advances at the time, and he even wrote books about his views on Cubism. Franz Marc was one of the earliest artists to participate in Cubism; his paintings used flat angles and abstract shapes to resemble broken mirrors [3]. 

Cubism allowed artists to express the beauty and emotion of imagination onto canvases. The geometric shapes showed off simplicity, the bright colors gave a sense of feeling, and the disjointed angles helped the figures stand out to the viewer. It would later inspire other art movements such as Futurism and Abstract Expressionism, as well as artists of other forms such as author James Joyce and musician Igor Stravinsky [1].
