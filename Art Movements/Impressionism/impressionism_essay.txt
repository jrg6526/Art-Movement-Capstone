Since the Industrial Revolution, middle- and low-class civilians in Paris could easily spend luxury time around the countrysides via railroads. Starting around 1860, a small group of artists – which included Claude Monet, Pierre-Auguste Renoir, and Alfred Sisley [1] - experimented with thick brush strokes and opposite colors to make lustrous, lifeful artworks depicting the outdoors [1][2][3]. Unable to submit their avant-garde works to the <i>Academie des Beaux-Arts</i>, the group created the <i>Cooperative and Anonymous Association of Painters, Sculptors and Engravers</i> to display their art [3]. 

The techniques of creating Impressionist art can lie in painting <i>en plein air</i> (in open air) [3]. Artists used simple strokes to depict outlines, complementary colors to simulate shadows in nature, and extra layers of solid colors on the canvas [1]. Some paintings, such as those of Edgar Degas, captured the appearance of a photograph, with the illusion of asymmetrical figures paused in movement [1][3]. These cropped paintings were inspired by Japanese ukiyo-e art, which depicted movement in a similar style [1]. 

Prior to this, popular artworks had a more realistic style and contained figures of historical or religious significance. The Impressionists’ artworks were considered unconventional at the time, and reviews from critics were initially mixed [3]. In fact, the coining of the term “Impressionism” was influenced by a satirical review written by art critic Louis Leroy, saying Monet’s <i>Impression, Sunrise</i> looked more unfinished than a sketch template for wallpaper [1][3]. 

Famous Impressionists included Frederic Bazille, Edgar Degas, Mary Cassatt, and Camille Pissarro. Bazille - an impactful figure in the movement who later died in the Franco-Prussian War [2] – painted humans in natural settings, such as a group of formally dressed people in the shade of a tree. Many of Degas’s paintings and sculptures depicted ballerinas, one of the sculptures wearing a real tutu and ribbon. Cassatt had artworks of children, especially depicted in relationships with their mothers. Pissarro painted landscapes of cities and countrysides in France, and he was a trainer for Paul Cezanne [2], whose later paintings would inspire Georges Braque’s Cubist art [4]. 

Impressionism revealed the beauty of nature and interactivity with free brush strokes and contrasting colors. Lower class artists freely painted natural landscapes and human interactions in contrast to detailed artworks with allegorical or religious undertones. Impressionism was one of the earliest modernist art movements in history; the Industrial Revolution and the railroad were modern milestones that influenced Impressionist artists [1]. The movement would later inspire Post-Impressionism and Abstract Expressionism [3].
